Why carriers run reefers

Refrigerated trailers haul freight that must stay within a set temperature range: produce, meat, dairy, frozen food, beverages and pharmaceuticals. The demand is steady because people buy these products every week.

Reefer freight often pays more per mile than dry van, but the trailer costs more to run, with reefer fuel and unit maintenance, and it carries more responsibility if the temperature goes out of range. The number that matters is net profit after reefer costs, not the headline rate.

What a reefer dispatcher checks on every load

  • Temperature range and mode (continuous or cycle) written clearly on the rate confirmation.
  • Pre-cool instructions and who pays for the extra fuel and time.
  • Appointment windows. Perishable freight is time-critical, and late arrival can mean rejection.
  • Reefer fuel and who is responsible for it.
  • Detention and layover terms, because a cooling trailer waiting at a dock costs money.
  • Record requirements, such as temperature logs or photos of the unit display at pickup and delivery.

Protecting yourself on reefer loads

Most reefer disputes come from missing records. A good habit is to photograph the trailer temperature at pickup and delivery, keep the signed BOL and keep reefer unit downloads. If a receiver questions the temperature, tell your dispatcher immediately so the broker and shipper can be contacted before the load is rejected.

Food-grade freight can also require a clean, odor-free trailer, so confirm wash-out expectations before you accept.
