Why dry van is the most flexible equipment

A dry van is an enclosed trailer that protects freight from weather and theft. Because most boxed, packaged and palletized freight moves in vans, dry van drivers see the biggest pool of loads, the most brokers and shippers, and the easiest reloads in most markets.

That also means more competition for every load. With so much freight available, rate and lane choice matter more than simply finding a load. The goal is not to stay busy, it is to stay busy on the right freight.

  • Typical dry van freight: consumer goods, retail replenishment, paper and packaging, non-perishable food, auto parts and general palletized freight.

What a dry van dispatcher does for you

  • Screens every load for rate per mile, deadhead miles and the broker's payment history before it reaches you.
  • Plans the reload before you deliver, so you are not left empty in a weak market.
  • Negotiates the rate and accessorials such as detention, layover and truck-ordered-not-used (TONU).
  • Handles the paperwork: rate confirmations, BOLs and invoicing.
  • Watches appointments and keeps brokers updated so you are not on the phone while driving.

Dry van details that decide your profit

Two loads can pay the same per mile and earn very different weeks. These are the factors a good dispatcher checks first:

  • Live load or drop-and-hook. Live loading can mean hours of waiting. Detention terms should be agreed before you accept.
  • Lumper and unloading fees. Know who pays before you arrive.
  • Freight density by region. Some areas have strong outbound freight and weak return freight, so the best plan is often a round trip, not a single load.
  • Weight. A 53-foot van typically carries up to about 45,000 lb of freight inside the 80,000 lb federal gross weight limit.
